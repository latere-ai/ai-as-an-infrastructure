Diffusion and Flow Matching

Author
Changkun Ou
Updated
June 21, 2026
Reading time
~7 min

Everything in this book so far generates by predicting the next token. The image, audio, and video models that Chapter 60 gestures at mostly do not: they start from pure noise and remove it. This chapter teaches that family, diffusion and its flow-matching generalization, because it is the generative principle behind almost all non-text media and is now reaching back into text. By the end a reader can explain why generation-by-denoising sidesteps the left-to-right constraint of autoregression, how a fixed noising process defines a learned reverse one, why training reduces to predicting noise, what made latent diffusion affordable, how flow matching reframed the whole thing as learning a velocity field, and why the frontier is collapsing a thousand sampling steps toward one.

Why not autoregression

Autoregressive generation is sequential by construction: one token after another, left to right, a serial chain whose length is the output. For text that ordering is natural. For an image it is not: pixels have no canonical order, and forcing one wastes the structure. Diffusion takes a different route. Corrupt data into noise by a fixed, known process, then learn to reverse it, denoising the whole signal in parallel at each step. There is no left-to-right, the output size is fixed up front, and the model can attend to the entire partially-denoised signal at once.

The forward and reverse processes

The forward process gradually adds Gaussian noise to a datum over TTT steps until little but noise remains, a fixed Markov chain with no parameters. Generation runs it backwards, learning to undo one noising step at a time. The surprise of Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models is that this reverse process has a simple trainable form: the network is trained to predict the noise ϵ\boldsymbol{\epsilon}ϵ that was added to a sample at a randomly chosen step (Ho et al. 2020), an ordinary regression,

Lsimple=E t, x0, ϵ∥ϵ−ϵθ ⁣(αˉt x0+1−αˉt ϵ,  t)∥2,\mathcal{L}_{\text{simple}} = \mathbb{E}_{\,t,\,\mathbf{x}_0,\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}} \left\lVert \boldsymbol{\epsilon} - \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta\!\left( \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\; t \right) \right\rVert^2, Lsimple​=Et,x0​,ϵ​​ϵ−ϵθ​(αˉt​​x0​+1−αˉt​​ϵ,t)​2,

where αˉt\bar{\alpha}_tαˉt​ is the surviving signal fraction at step ttt. Sampling starts from pure noise and applies the learned denoiser step by step. The toy below makes the loop concrete: for one-dimensional Gaussian data the optimal noise-predictor is analytic, so we can sample from noise and watch the distribution come back.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

mu, v0 = 3.0, 1.0                          # data ~ N(mu, v0)
T = 1000
beta = np.linspace(1e-4, 0.02, T)         # noise schedule
alpha = 1 - beta
abar = np.cumprod(alpha)                   # surviving signal fraction

def eps_star(x, t):                        # optimal epsilon-prediction (analytic)
    var = abar[t] * v0 + (1 - abar[t])
    return np.sqrt(1 - abar[t]) * (x - np.sqrt(abar[t]) * mu) / var

x = rng.normal(size=5000)                  # start from pure noise
for t in range(T - 1, -1, -1):
    mean = (x - beta[t] / np.sqrt(1 - abar[t]) * eps_star(x, t)) / np.sqrt(alpha[t])
    x = mean + (np.sqrt(beta[t]) * rng.normal(size=x.shape) if t > 0 else 0.0)

print(f"data N(mu={mu}, var={v0});  samples: mean={x.mean():.2f}, var={x.var():.2f}")

One framework: scores and SDEs

A second lineage arrived at the same place from a different direction. Score-based models learn the score, the gradient of the log-density, at many noise levels and sample by Langevin dynamics (Song and Ermon 2019). Song et al. then showed the two are one thing: the forward process is a stochastic differential equation that carries data to noise, generation is the reverse-time SDE, and both DDPM and score-matching are discretizations of it (Song et al. 2021). The same work derives an equivalent deterministic probability-flow ODE with the same marginals, and that ODE is the bridge to fast samplers and to flow matching below.

Making it cheap and steerable

Three moves turned a slow research method into a deployed one. Fewer steps came first: DDIM is a non-Markovian sampler that reaches comparable quality 10 to 50 times faster, and is deterministic when its noise term is set to zero (Song et al. 2021). Affordability came from latent diffusion, which runs the whole process in the latent space of a pretrained autoencoder rather than on pixels, cutting compute by a large factor, and injects text or layout through cross-attention, the recipe behind Stable Diffusion (Rombach et al. 2022). Control came from classifier-free guidance: train one model jointly with and without the conditioning signal by dropping it part of the time, then at sampling extrapolate between the conditional and unconditional predictions by a guidance weight, trading diversity for fidelity with no separate classifier (Ho and Salimans 2022). The backbone then caught up with the rest of the book: DiT replaces the U-Net with a transformer over latent patches and finds that sample quality falls as compute rises, putting diffusion on the same scaling curve as everything else (Peebles and Xie 2023).

Flow matching: learn a velocity, not a denoiser

Flow matching reframes the problem. Instead of a denoiser, learn a vector field that transports noise to data along a fixed conditional probability path, trained simulation-free by regressing onto that field, with the diffusion paths falling out as one special case and straight optimal-transport paths as a more efficient other (Lipman et al. 2023). Rectified flow pushes the idea to its useful extreme: learn straight-line transport between noise and data, which lets an Euler sampler take very few steps, even one, and a recursive "rectification" keeps straightening the trajectories (Liu et al. 2023). Consistency models collapse the path entirely, training a network that maps any point on the probability-flow ODE trajectory directly to its origin, so one forward pass generates a sample, either distilled from a diffusion teacher or trained standalone (Song et al. 2023). The arc across this section is a single quantity coming down: the number of sequential network evaluations a sample costs.

The loop back to language

Diffusion is now returning to text. LLaDA trains a language model from scratch with a masked-denoising diffusion objective, predicting masked tokens by a reverse process rather than left to right, and reports it competitive with an autoregressive LLaMA3-8B at in-context learning (Nie and others 2025). The appeal is parallel, bidirectional decoding and an escape from strict left-to-right order; the open question is whether the approach scales to the frontier.

What's contested

Whether non-autoregressive diffusion language models can match autoregressive LLMs at scale is unsettled. LLaDA shows competitiveness at 8B, but against the authors' own controlled baselines rather than external frontier models, and the strongest "surpasses" result is narrow, a reversal task GPT-4o handles poorly (Nie and others 2025). The case for diffusion text rests on parallel decoding and bidirectional context; the case against is that autoregression still owns every frontier model and has the more mature scaling and serving story. Treat diffusion for language as promising and unproven above small scale.

Constraint arrow

The sampler's step count is a serving-cost lever that reaches back into the training objective. A thousand-step DDPM is too slow to serve at scale, so the field trains specifically for few-step sampling, rectified flow's straight paths and consistency distillation, rather than treating sampling as an afterthought. The inference budget per image, paid on every generation, dictates which generative objective is worth training, the same constraint Chapter 3 draws for tokens, here drawn for denoising steps.
