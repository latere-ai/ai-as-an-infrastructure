Part V · Chapter 28

Embeddings and Representation Learning

Author
Changkun Ou
Updated
June 21, 2026
Reading time
~7 min

The retrieval of Chapter 27 leaned on a model that maps a query and a chunk into one vector space, a dual-encoder, with a cross-encoder reranker and ColBERT's late interaction as the more expressive cousins. That chapter used those models. It did not say how they come to place related text near each other. This chapter does. By the end a reader can explain why an embedding model is trained against a contrastive objective rather than next-token prediction, why the choice of negatives is the central design variable, how one model serves many tasks and many vector sizes, and how the strongest 2025 embedders are bootstrapped from large language models.

A geometry you can compare

A language model is trained to predict the next token, and nothing in that objective asks two texts that mean the same thing to land near each other. Its hidden states carry meaning, but the space is not metric: cosine distance in it does not track relatedness, so pooling a decoder's states gives a mediocre retriever. Retrieval needs the opposite of generation. It needs a space where a single distance, computed once and indexed, ranks a million chunks against a query. The embedding model exists to learn that space, and it is trained for it directly rather than borrowed from a generator.

The contrastive objective

The principle is to pull a positive pair together and push negatives apart. InfoNCE, introduced for contrastive predictive coding (Oord et al. 2018), scores a query qqq against its positive d+d^{+}d+ and a set of negatives {dj−}\{d^{-}_j\}{dj−​} as a softmax over similarities, with a temperature τ\tauτ:

L=−log⁡exp⁡(sim⁡(q,d+)/τ)exp⁡(sim⁡(q,d+)/τ)+∑jexp⁡(sim⁡(q,dj−)/τ)\mathcal{L} = -\log \frac{\exp(\operatorname{sim}(q, d^{+})/\tau)} {\exp(\operatorname{sim}(q, d^{+})/\tau) + \sum_j \exp(\operatorname{sim}(q, d^{-}_j)/\tau)} L=−logexp(sim(q,d+)/τ)+∑j​exp(sim(q,dj−​)/τ)exp(sim(q,d+)/τ)​

The model learns by discrimination, not reconstruction: it never has to generate the document, only to rank the right one above the wrong ones. Sentence-BERT made this practical for whole sentences with a siamese dual-encoder whose outputs compare by cosine (Reimers and Gurevych 2019), against the alternative of a BERT cross-encoder that is accurate but must run on every pair and so cannot scan a corpus (the same bi-encoder-versus-cross-encoder split Chapter 27 draws at retrieval time, here decided at training time).

Negatives are the design variable

The positive is given; the skill is in what you push away. Drawing negatives at random teaches only coarse distinctions, so two moves dominate the recipes. The first is in-batch negatives: Dense Passage Retrieval treats the other positives in a training batch as negatives for each query, so a larger batch buys more negatives almost for free and a bigger batch directly raises retrieval quality (Karpukhin et al. 2020). The second is hard negatives, wrong-but-plausible passages that force a finer boundary, usually mined with a cheap lexical retriever. RocketQA sharpens both, adding cross-batch negatives and denoising the hard negatives, because a passage retrieved as a negative is sometimes actually relevant, a false negative that punishes the model for being right (Qu and others 2021).

When labeled pairs are scarce, augmentation manufactures a positive. SimCSE makes its positive pair by encoding the same sentence twice under different dropout masks, an almost-free perturbation that works surprisingly well, and its supervised form draws positives from natural-language-inference entailment pairs (Gao et al. 2021).

Average the loss over many queries as the negatives are drawn closer to the query, and watch it climb.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
d = 32

def infonce(q, dpos, dnegs, tau=0.05):
    sims = np.array([q @ dpos] + [q @ x for x in dnegs]) / tau
    sims -= sims.max()                        # numerical stability
    p = np.exp(sims) / np.exp(sims).sum()
    return -np.log(p[0])                       # loss = -log P(positive)

def unit(v): return v / np.linalg.norm(v)

def mean_loss(hardness, n, trials=300):        # hardness 0=random, ->1 toward query
    total = 0.0
    for _ in range(trials):
        q = unit(rng.normal(size=d))
        dpos = unit(q + 0.4 * rng.normal(size=d))
        negs = [unit(hardness * q + (1 - hardness) * rng.normal(size=d)) for _ in range(n)]
        total += infonce(q, dpos, negs)
    return total / trials

for h in [0.0, 0.3, 0.6]:
    print(f"negative hardness {h:.1f}: mean loss = {mean_loss(h, 16):.3f}")
print("harder negatives (closer to the query) -> larger loss -> stronger signal")

One model, many tasks and many sizes

A single embedder is asked to serve search, clustering, classification, and deduplication at once, and two ideas stretch one model across that range. The first conditions the embedding on a task instruction: Instructor prepends a short description of the task so the same weights produce a query embedding tuned for retrieval or for similarity without per-task fine-tuning (Su and others 2022). The second makes the vector size itself a dial. Matryoshka representation learning trains so that the first mmm dimensions of the embedding are themselves a usable embedding, as accurate as one trained natively at mmm, at no additional training cost (Kusupati and others 2022). You store the full vector once and truncate it per query, trading accuracy for index cost without retraining.

Where the training pairs come from

The bottleneck is rarely the loss; it is the supply of query-document pairs. The weakly-supervised route mines them at scale: E5 pretrains contrastively on hundreds of millions of naturally occurring pairs, posts and comments, titles and bodies, before a small supervised fine-tune (Wang et al. 2022). The route that now leads instead bootstraps from a generator. E5-mistral prompts a large language model to synthesize diverse retrieval tasks and their query-document pairs, then fine-tunes Mistral-7B itself into the embedder, reaching the top of the benchmarks with little human-labeled data (Wang et al. 2023). The generative model trains the retrieval model, a clean instance of the synthetic-data loop of Chapter 12. A third route distills the cross-encoder of Chapter 27 into a bi-encoder, transferring the accuracy of full query-document interaction into the cost of a single dot product.

What's contested

Whether one general embedder can dominate is unsettled. MTEB, which scores embedders across classification, clustering, retrieval, reranking, and semantic similarity, found that no single model wins every task, so a leaderboard number hides which task it was won on (Muennighoff et al. 2022). One camp reads this as temporary, a stronger LLM-bootstrapped embedder will generalize across all of them; the other reads it as structural, because the geometry that separates topics for clustering is not the geometry that ranks answers for retrieval, which is exactly why instruction-conditioning exists. Treat an embedding leaderboard score as task-shaped until tested on your own retrieval distribution.

Constraint arrow

The vector index reaches back into how the representation is trained. The serving cost of retrieval scales with the embedding dimension times the corpus size (Chapter 27), and that cost, paid on every query forever, is what justifies training for truncatability in the first place: Matryoshka exists because a 1024-dimensional vector over a billion chunks is expensive enough that being able to drop to 256 dimensions without retraining is worth designing for. The retrieval layer's cost dictates the shape of the vector the training run is asked to produce.
