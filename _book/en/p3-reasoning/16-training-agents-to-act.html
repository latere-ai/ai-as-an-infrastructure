Part III · Chapter 17

Training Agents to Act

Author
Changkun Ou
Updated
June 21, 2026
Reading time
~5 min

The reasoning models of Chapter 15 are trained to produce a single answer: one prompt, one chain, one check. An agent does more. It takes many turns, calls tools, and reads what comes back before it is done. This chapter extends the verifiable-reward recipe from rewarding an answer to rewarding a whole trajectory, and covers the systems that make that loop run. By the end a reader can explain why a rollout becomes an agent's full interaction with an environment, why the environment is now a training asset in its own right, why credit assignment is the hard part and stays unsettled, and how a training run comes to contain a serving engine.

Rewarding a trajectory, not an answer

The machinery carries over, GRPO or PPO over sampled rollouts against a verifiable reward (Chapter 15), but a rollout is now an agent's full interaction with an environment rather than one completion. This is where the training loop reaches into the agentic loop of Chapter 26: the harness that runs an agent at inference becomes, pointed at a checkable environment, the thing that generates training trajectories.

The early exemplars are domain-shaped. WebGPT pointed here as far back as 2021, fine-tuning a model to browse and gather references, though its best system used behavior cloning and rejection sampling rather than full RL (Nakano et al. 2021). Search-R1 trains a model to interleave reasoning with live search queries across a multi-turn trajectory, rewarded only by whether the final answer matches and with the retrieved tokens masked out of the loss (Jin et al. 2025). ToolRL applies GRPO to general tool use and reports that a coarse answer-matching reward is not enough, so it shapes a finer reward over the tool-call steps (Qian et al. 2025). SWE-RL scales the idea to software engineering, rewarding a patch by its similarity to the developer's real fix mined from open-source history (Wei et al. 2025).

Environments as the training substrate

The environment becomes a training asset in its own right. SWE-Gym packages real repositories with executable runtimes and unit tests so an agent's trajectory can be sampled and scored in the loop, the unit test serving as the verifier (Pan et al. 2024), which is the sandbox of Chapter 50 turned from a place to run an agent into a place to train one. The shift is large enough that environments are now collected and sold as products, a 2025 market of training grounds for agents (Zeff 2025), though the value of any single environment is contested, since a model that masters a skill can generate cheap substitute data for it (Anderson 2025).

Credit assignment over a trajectory

What does not carry over cleanly is credit assignment. An outcome reward at the end of a long trajectory cannot say which tool call or which turn earned or lost the run, and that sparse, delayed signal is where multi-turn RL gets hard. RAGEN makes the difficulty concrete with StarPO, a trajectory-level method that optimizes the whole reasoning-and-action sequence: without a finer signal, training tends to fall into what its authors call an Echo Trap, collapsing onto a few locally rewarded patterns as gradients spike and entropy drops (Wang and others 2025).

What's contested

Whether outcome-only rewards suffice for agentic tasks is unsettled. Search-R1 and ToolRL report strong results from outcome or lightly-shaped rewards in narrow domains, while RAGEN finds that reasoning fails to emerge without reasoning-aware credit in stylized ones (Wang and others 2025). This is the outcome-versus-process tension of Chapter 15, now stretched over a trajectory instead of a chain, and which way it resolves looks domain-dependent and tied to how dense a signal the environment can supply, rather than settled by a single best answer.

The systems split: generation and learning

An agentic RL loop generates rollouts from the current policy, scores them, and updates the weights, and generation is the bottleneck, so modern toolkits embed an inference engine, almost always vLLM, to produce the rollouts (Chapter 49). The training box now contains a serving box, and two architectural patterns split its compute. The colocated pattern keeps the trainer and the rollout engine on the same GPUs and reshards the model between them: HybridFlow, whose open implementation is veRL, reshards the actor between its training and generation layouts with what it calls a 3D-HybridEngine, reusing the training weights for generation so no memory is duplicated (Sheng et al. 2025). The disaggregated pattern puts the actor, reward, reference, and critic models on separate GPU pools and schedules them with Ray: OpenRLHF takes this route, embedding vLLM for the rollout phase that dominates RLHF wall-clock, by its own measure around 80 percent of it (Hu and others 2024). The choice is the familiar one between packing for utilization and disaggregating for scheduling flexibility, now made inside a single training run.

The deeper knob is how fresh the rollouts must be. Strict on-policy RL waits for the latest weights before each batch, which leaves the trainer idle while the slower generator runs. Asynchronous RL lets generation run ahead on slightly stale weights and overlaps the two, trading a controlled amount of off-policy staleness for throughput, the same staleness-against-utilization trade the pipeline schedules of Chapter 9 make at the gradient level.
