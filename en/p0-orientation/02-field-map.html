2  A Field Map and How to Read This Book

A book this wide is easy to get lost in. This chapter is the map and the compass you carry through the rest of it. By the end of it a reader can place any topic in the book on a single picture, say which parts of that picture are settled and which are still argued over, see where this book sits next to the comprehensive titles already on the shelf, and read everything that follows design-first: asking of every component why it has the shape it has, not merely what it does.

It is organized around four questions a newcomer to the field actually asks. Where does each piece go? Which parts can I trust as fact and which are live argument? What does this book give me that others do not? And how do I read it without drowning?

2.1 One map, nine parts

sec-whole-stack walked one task from raw data to a deployed agent in a single pass. This chapter zooms out from that vertical slice to the whole field and shows how the nine substantive parts, Part I through Part IX, interlock. The cleanest way to hold them in mind is as a stack: the lifecycle of a capability, with compute and data at the bottom, a governed behavior at the top, and at every layer a set of choices that the layer below constrains and the layer above inherits. Figure fig-field-map-stack draws that stack.

Read the solid arrows first; they are the build order. Part VII, the accelerators, the networks, and the data infrastructure, sits at the bottom: it is drawn below Part I because a cluster’s shape is the first constraint on what can be trained at all (sec-accelerators-networking, sec-orchestration-data-infra). Part I turns that compute into a base model through scaling laws, data curation, tokenization, architecture, and training at scale (sec-scaling-laws, sec-data-curation, sec-tokenization, sec-transformer-architecture, sec-moe-ssm-hybrids, sec-training-at-scale). Part II adapts the base model to follow instructions and to human preference (sec-sft-peft, sec-rlhf, sec-dpo-variants, sec-synthetic-data). Part III teaches it to spend test-time compute on reasoning (sec-eliciting-reasoning, sec-training-to-reason, sec-inference-time-scaling). Part IV is the serving problem: turning a trained model into tokens a user can afford to wait for (sec-serving-problem, sec-memory-scheduling, sec-faster-decoding, sec-quantization-kernels, sec-structured-long-context). Part V composes served models into agents with memory, tools, retrieval, and context (sec-agent-architectures, sec-memory-systems, sec-the-harness, sec-multi-agent-systems, sec-rag-retrieval, sec-context-engineering). Part VI measures whether any of it works (sec-benchmarks, sec-judging-holistic, sec-evaluating-agents). Part VIII reaches sideways into every layer to ask whether the behavior can be understood, overseen, and secured (sec-interpretability, sec-oversight-control, sec-security-authorization). Part IX prices the whole thing and surveys who builds what (sec-model-landscape, sec-tooling-ecosystem, sec-economics).

The dashed arrows are the reason to read in order, and they are easy to miss. They do not feed one layer into the next; they reach across the stack. Evaluation feeds back into adaptation: a benchmark or a judge that moves changes what the next round of post-training optimizes for. Safety constrains adaptation and orchestration rather than sitting at the end as a coat of paint. Economics prices pretraining and serving, and that price reaches up the stack to change design choices that look, locally, like pure modeling decisions. Hold the dashed arrows in mind as you read; the chapter on reading instructions below shows the sharpest one in full.

2.2 Solid ground and swamp

A map is only useful if it marks the solid ground separately from the swamp, and a reader’s first defense against the noise of this field is knowing which is which.

Some of this book is settled engineering. The transformer block and its attention mechanism (Vaswani et al. 2017), the autoregressive next-token objective, attention with a key-value cache, mixed-precision training, and the basic fact that loss falls as a smooth power law in compute (Kaplan et al. 2020) are no longer in dispute; they are the shared substrate every lab builds on. A reader can learn them as one learns the memory model of a language: as the rules of the game, fixed for the duration of play.

Much of the frontier is not settled, and the book marks those places rather than papering over them. The compute-optimal ratio of tokens to parameters is a budget-and-deployment-dependent choice, not a constant (Hoffmann et al. 2022) (sec-scaling-laws). Whether reinforcement learning on verifiable rewards teaches genuinely new reasoning or only sharpens what pretraining already put there is argued in sec-training-to-reason, where work like DeepSeek-R1 makes the case that pure reinforcement learning can incentivize reasoning behavior (Guo et al. 2025). Whether sparse autoencoders are the right primitive for reading a model’s internal features, or a convenient but lossy lens, is open in sec-interpretability. Whether a benchmark number or the harness that runs it is the thing actually being measured runs through sec-benchmarks and sec-evaluating-agents. The signature of the book is that these live debates get a box, with named positions, instead of a confident sentence that hides the disagreement.

ImportantWhat’s contested

The deepest open questions cluster in the upper and outer layers of the map, and they are exactly the white space this book is written to occupy. Three are worth naming now. First, interpretability has no agreed primitive: the superposition hypothesis gave the field a way to think about why a model packs more features than it has dimensions (Elhage et al. 2022), and sparse autoencoders gave a tractable handle on it (Bricken et al. 2023), but whether the features they recover are the model’s real computational units or an artifact of the method is unresolved, and sec-interpretability treats it as open. Second, the source of reasoning gains is contested: sec-training-to-reason weighs the claim that reinforcement learning elicits latent capability against the claim that it installs new capability (Guo et al. 2025), because the two imply different research bets. Third, agent architecture has no settled form: whether long-horizon competence comes from better memory systems (sec-memory-systems), a stronger single-agent harness (sec-the-harness), or genuine multi-agent decomposition (sec-multi-agent-systems) is an empirical question the field has not closed. Treat any confident answer in these three areas as a position, not a fact.

2.3 Three books, and the gap between them

A reader deciding where to spend attention deserves to know what this book is not. Three comprehensive books already cover much of this territory well, and this book is written to complement them rather than to repeat them. Naming them precisely is the fastest way to say what is new here.

Chip Huyen’s AI Engineering (Huyen 2025) is the application layer done thoroughly: how to build products on top of foundation models you call rather than train, covering evaluation, prompt and context design, retrieval, and deployment from the perspective of an engineer adapting models to a use case. It treats the model largely as a given and asks how to build well around it.

Sebastian Raschka’s Build a Large Language Model (From Scratch) (Raschka 2024) is the laptop-scale build: it implements a GPT-style model end to end in PyTorch, from tokenization through attention to pretraining and fine-tuning, so a reader understands the machine by constructing a small one. Its gift is mechanical clarity at a scale one person can run.

Suhas Pai’s Designing Large Language Model Applications (Pai 2025) is the broadest of the three, a holistic tour from the ingredients of a language model through fine-tuning, retrieval, and agents. It covers a wide surface, and by design it is lighter on the parts that only appear at frontier scale: at-scale distributed training, the internals of a production serving stack, and mechanistic interpretability.

The white space those three leave open is the middle and lower stack at frontier scale. This book runs whole-stack, from the cluster to the agent, and spends its depth where the others are thin: distributed training across thousands of accelerators (sec-training-at-scale, sec-accelerators-networking), the internals of serving, what actually happens inside the loop that turns a model into affordable tokens (sec-serving-problem, sec-memory-scheduling, sec-faster-decoding), and interpretability as a first-class engineering concern rather than a research footnote (sec-interpretability). The aim is not to argue the other books are wrong anywhere; it is to read the layers they pass over quickly, slowly and design-first.

2.4 Reading instructions

The map tells you where things are. This last section tells you how each chapter is built, so the reading goes faster.

Every substantial chapter moves through the same arc. A chapter opens with the Problem: the essential constraint the component answers, in terms of latency, cost, memory, scale, safety, or correctness. It then gives the Design: the core idea that answers the problem and the principle underneath it. Evolution traces how the design got here, which earlier approaches came first and why they were superseded, through primary sources. Trade-offs names what is sacrificed for what, and where the known gaps are. Implementation appears only when a minimal sketch, a config, or an operational detail clarifies the design, never to reproduce a file. Each chapter closes with Further reading, first-hand sources first. The arc carries an engineering-to-theory motion: the practice is established first, then the foundations that explain it.

Two callouts carry the book’s intellectual signature, and learning to read them is worth the small effort. A What’s contested box marks where the field genuinely disagrees, with named positions, as in the box above. A Constraint arrow box marks where a lower layer dictates an upper layer’s choice. These arrows are the payoff of reading the stack in order rather than dipping into chapters at random, and the next box shows the canonical one.

TipConstraint arrow

The clearest single instance of a constraint arrow runs from serving up to pretraining. sec-scaling-laws decides how large a model to train and on how many tokens, and read locally that looks like pure training economics: minimize loss for a fixed compute budget, the compute-optimal recipe (Hoffmann et al. 2022). But if a model will serve an enormous volume of tokens over its life, the right move is to deliberately over-train a smaller model far past the compute-optimal point, because training is paid once and inference is paid forever. The decision is set by the serving cost in sec-serving-problem reaching up the stack, not by the training budget alone. When you meet a choice that looks unmotivated inside its own chapter, look one layer down: the arrow that explains it usually starts there.

Two motifs recur without needing a callout, and noticing them ties distant chapters together. The three loops, training, inference, and agentic, are one control structure seen at three timescales: a weeks-long loop that turns compute into a model, a millisecond loop that turns a prompt into tokens, and a multi-step loop that turns a goal into actions. Figure fig-field-map-three-loops draws all three side by side so the shared shape is visible: each is an input that feeds a step that produces an output that returns to the input, and only the payload and the timescale differ.

The capability, efficiency, trust lens closes many chapters: a technique buys capability, or efficiency, or trust, and usually trades one against another, and naming which it buys keeps the comparison honest.

A reader does not have to read strictly front to back. The parts are written to interlock, so following the solid arrows of the map, pretraining before adaptation, serving before orchestration, gives the constraint arrows their force and is the recommended path. Reading by interest is fine too; the cross-references will point back to whatever lower layer a chapter depends on. Either way, keep the four questions of this chapter in hand: where it goes on the map, whether it is solid or contested, what this book adds, and which way the arrows run.
